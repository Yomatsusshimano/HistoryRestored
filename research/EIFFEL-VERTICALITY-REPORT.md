# An inspection during assembly

Research draft, 2026-10-08; no independent review.

An eight-page manuscript in Archives nationales **F/12/3896**, [provided by the archive through Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rapport_sur_la_v%C3%A9rification_de_la_verticalit%C3%A9_de_la_tour_Eiffel_1889.pdf), was downloaded and all pages visually inspected. Its first page dates the report to **4 January 1889**. The title and diagrams concern checking the tower's verticality. This is a different document and administrative context from Eiffel's 1900 retrospective monograph, although the inspection involved a representative of Eiffel and is not wholly independent of the project.

## Observations and limits

| PDF page | Source report | What it establishes or leaves open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Diagram and text describe the tower at approximately 220 m on 4 January 1889; point f is on an upper member beginning panel 22 | A dated description of an intermediate structure, not a completed 300 m tower |
| 2–4 | A theodolite checks selected face midlines; stations lie roughly 160–300 m from the axis; obstacles prevent using the intended ground points, so paired structural points establish sighting planes | A described survey procedure with explicit substitutions, not raw modern instrument data |
| 4–5 | At the Pont d'Iéna station, point f lies on a member not yet riveted, only bolted or pinned | Specific assembly work remained; this challenges an account of an unchanged, already completed tower |
| 5 | Station beta has trees, a theatre roof and difficult lighting. Points b and d appear approximately 20 mm and 10 mm left; e and f are hidden | Nonzero and missing observations are retained; hidden points are not zero deviations |
| 6 | At station gamma, the observers deliberately shift the instrument about 300 mm outside the principal plane and see an apparent shift of about 50 mm at b | A reported sensitivity demonstration, not actual tower movement or a full uncertainty calibration |
| 7–8 | The report concludes vertical alignment over about 180 m between a and f, with a qualification concerning beta, then asserts absolute verticality | This is the authors' conclusion. Their alignment assumptions and finite visibility do not justify treating our transcription as proof of mathematically exact verticality |

The final page has separate signature blocks for the control inspector and the engineer representing Eiffel. These establish stated roles; this audit does not independently authenticate signatures or expand uncertain handwritten names. No complete diplomatic transcription has been made.

## Implication for the construction claim

This is more discriminating than a later caption or a budget total: it reports a partially assembled tower, identifies an unfinished connection, and explains how observers tested alignment. It supports recorded assembly at the stated date. It does **not**, by itself, distinguish newly fabricated components from reused ones, date foundations, verify the full manufacturing budget, or prove that every historical attribution is correct. Those questions still require supplier and material evidence.

The archive's modern wind-drawing article was also checked, but its displayed image is cropped to the upper tower; the caption's 1886 date was not independently read on that crop. It has not been promoted to a scanned-date verification.

Next compare the January inspection with dated erection diagrams and photographs, and link the reported panel/member to original part registers. Preserve the beta observations when assessing the report's strong concluding language.

## Comparison with the 8 January 1888 erection diagram

The archive-provided [erection diagram, F/12/3871](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tour_Eiffel,_diagramme_de_montage_1888.jpg) (S151) was visually inspected as a whole and at enlarged scale for its lower caption and legend. The sheet itself identifies the state of progress through **8 January 1888**, agreeing with the catalog date. The drawing shows four numbered support groups, connecting lattice girders and a direction arrow for the Seine. It is not a photograph, surveyed map or full-height elevation.

The handwritten legend assigns light tint to iron assembled but not riveted, and dark tint to iron both assembled and riveted. It also states that the stairs are assembled and riveted up to the third diagonal brace. These are reported construction states, not merely proposed pieces. Untinted lines have not been assigned a status here: the inspected legend does not explicitly define them. No member counts or percent-completion estimate have been inferred from colored area.

Together, S151 and S150 document two different reported assembly states approximately a year apart. The earlier diagram records selective assembly/riveting; the later inspection describes the roughly 220 m structure and an upper connection still awaiting riveting. This is compatible with progressive erection and challenges the specific claim that the tower was already complete and unchanged throughout that interval. It does not resolve whether individual pieces were newly made or reused, nor independently authenticate every sheet's date. Both records concern the same project and survive in the same ministerial archival series; they are not independent archaeological dates or two unrelated tests of a global chronology.

The comparison remains qualitative: the earlier sheet lacks a verified whole-tower height, and a member-by-member match to the later report has not been established. A numerical vertical construction rate would therefore be unsupported. Next retrieve intervening progress sheets and dated photographs, cross-identify members, and trace supplier receipts. ## A photograph between the two records

The earlier catalog-only lead S08 is now visually inspected: [Musée Carnavalet PH73441](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/en/node/587811), attributed by Paris Musées to Hippolyte Blancard. The museum describes a platinum print measuring 15 × 22.2 cm, on an older mount, acquired by manual gift associated with Maurice Rousseau. No acquisition date or complete ownership chain was recovered. These are catalog descriptions, not our physical examination of the print.

The museum's linked [IIIF manifest](https://apicollections.parismusees.paris.fr/iiif/320352593/manifest) supplied the larger reproduction. The lower mount label visibly reads a construction view dated **10 February 1888**, with numbers **721** and **Cl. 1298**; the right margin names the Seine and bears **6628**. This corroborates the catalog's transcription on the object reproduction. It does not independently establish when the inscription was attached, when the negative was exposed, or when this particular print was made. The catalog mentions inscriptions on both sides; only the displayed front was inspected.

Visible content includes sloping lower legs, horizontal lattice girders linking them, and dense temporary support framing beneath. The upper shaft is absent from the depicted structure, with open sky above the connected lower frame. The river and embankment occupy the foreground. Individual rivets, exact support materials, member manufacture dates and a surveyed height cannot be resolved from this image. No pixel-based height or labor estimate is entered.

Placed by its inscription date, the photograph lies between the January 1888 progress diagram and January 1889 inspection. It adds a different evidence type and museum custody to the record of intermediate construction. The visual state is compatible with the progressive-erection account; a complete, unchanged tower is not what the image depicts. An inheritance or reuse explanation would need to account for that partial structure and the other dated records, while the new-manufacture account still requires supplier evidence. This remains a retrospective comparison, not a successful forecast or independent material chronology.
